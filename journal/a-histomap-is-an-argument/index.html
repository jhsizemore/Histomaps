A Histomap Is an Argument About History

Most timelines flatten history into a sequence of dates. A histomap starts from continuity. Instead of asking only when did this happen?, it asks what was flowing through time when it happened?

J. Hunter Sizemore
Histomaps.org

Published August 2026

Explore the first map →

A king dies. A war begins. An empire falls. A book is published. Each event receives a point on a line, and the line keeps moving.

But history does not actually arrive as a sequence of isolated points. States persist. Religions spread. Languages divide. Populations rise and fall. Institutions outlive the people who built them. A single human life runs for a few centimetres against structures that may run for centuries.

A histomap starts from that continuity.

Instead of asking only when did this happen?, it asks what was flowing through time when it happened?

That is the idea behind Histomaps.org.

Beginning with John B. Sparks

In 1931, John B. Sparks published a remarkable object called The Histomap: Four Thousand Years of World History. It was a tall, narrow chart — about 31 centimetres wide and 158 centimetres high — published by Histomap, Inc. and printed and distributed in the United States by Rand McNally. Its subtitle stated its argument plainly: “Relative Power of Contemporary States, Nations and Empires.”

Sparks drew history as coloured streams. Egypt widens and narrows. Greece emerges. Rome swells across the field. Empires divide, disappear and give way to others. The result is immediately legible in a way most timelines are not: you can look sideways across the map and see what existed at the same time, then look down and watch historical forms change.

The map is also unmistakably an argument. What counts as a civilization? Where does one end and another begin? What does “power” mean? How can the width of Rome be compared with the width of China? Sparks made those decisions, and the page turned them into geometry.

That is not a weakness of the form. It is the interesting part.

Histomap as a format

I am not trying to make a definitive digital edition of Sparks's chart.

My assertion is that a histomap is a format.

It is a way of arranging historical claims so that continuity, scale and contemporaneity become visible. Different Histomaps can make different arguments because their streams can represent different things.

A map of world civilizations might use width as a qualitative estimate of political power or influence. A population Histomap could use actual population. A map of domestication could show the diffusion and importance of wheat, rice, maize, cattle, horses, dogs and other species. A map of political movements might trace institutions, parties and ideologies through transformations that ordinary timelines make look like clean breaks.

The grammar is more important than any one dataset:

  • time runs continuously;
  • subjects persist as streams;
  • streams can grow, shrink, split, merge and end;
  • width has an explicitly defined meaning;
  • people can appear as lifelines inside larger historical currents;
  • events punctuate history without replacing its continuity;
  • and the reader should be able to see what else was happening at the same moment.

The current World Histomap is the first attempt to develop that grammar interactively.

Why keep the streams?

There have been several modern attempts to revisit Sparks's idea, and some are excellent timeline projects in their own right. But what keeps bringing me back to the original is the streams.

Without them, a Histomap becomes another historical timeline with coloured regions behind it. The streams are what allow the map to say something about persistence and transformation.

They also create possibilities that Sparks's fixed sheet of paper could not easily accommodate.

The current map still lives inside a bounded field. Ultimately, I want to remove that assumption. The sides should be able to expand organically so that the total width of the map can itself mean something.

Imagine a Histomap of human population. For most of history the field would be relatively narrow. It would gradually broaden, contract during major demographic catastrophes, and then expand with astonishing speed in the modern era. During the Black Death, the map itself could become visibly smaller.

That immediately changes the logic of the form. History no longer has to be a zero-sum game in which one stream can only grow because another shrinks. The total system can grow and contract too.

A life against history

One of the features I care most about in the interactive map is the lifeline.

A historical person is usually presented as a portrait, a biography or another event in a chronology. On a Histomap, a life is a duration. Aristotle is not simply “384–322 BCE” written in text. His life occupies a small vertical span inside a much larger Greek stream, alongside the political and cultural world in which he lived.

That scale is emotionally useful. A very consequential human life is still short.

As the map develops, lifelines can carry milestones, works, reigns, journeys and other events, while remaining subordinate to the broader history flowing around them.

The maps I want to make next

The point of Histomaps.org is that the World Histomap should eventually become one item in a library.

I live in the Pacific, and Pacific History is one of the maps I most want to make. World-history diagrams often push Oceania to the edge or omit it almost completely. A Pacific Histomap can reverse the perspective: migration, island societies, exchange networks, colonial powers, missions, labour movements, independence and regional institutions can become the central field rather than peripheral notes.

I also want to make a Histomap of the United States. That map can test something I find historically important: wars and constitutional moments do not create the clean breaks that textbook chapters imply. In particular, the end of the Civil War is not the end of the political, institutional and ideological streams that produced the Confederacy. Reconstruction, its failure, disenfranchisement, segregation and later political movements can be shown as continuities and transformations rather than a reset in 1865.

Domestication is another natural subject. Plants and animals have historical lives that cross borders and civilizations. Their streams would tell a history in which wheat, rice, maize, cattle, horses, pigs and dogs can be followed through the human societies they help reshape.

And then there are Histomaps of arguments. Big works of historical synthesis — books, documentaries, lecture series and podcasts — make claims about causation and relative importance. A Histomap can make the structure of such an argument visible enough to inspect and disagree with. That opens the possibility of collaborations with authors and creators rather than simply illustrating their work.

The map should be contestable

None of this works if the map presents itself as History with a capital H.

Every Histomap has to answer uncomfortable questions. Why is this stream here? Why is it this wide? Why did it change names? Why do two things merge? Why is one event shown and another missing?

Those are not bugs to conceal. They are the editorial work.

The long-term goal is to expose more of that reasoning: methodology, sources, confidence, disputed interpretations and alternate models. A Histomap should become more useful as a historical argument when you can see how it was made.

The first public map

Histomaps.org begins, appropriately, where the word began: with Sparks's World Histomap.

The current public preview adds zoom, interaction, historical events, people, lifelines and contextual information to the flowing field. It is not finished, and I do not want to pretend it is. The next stage depends on watching real people use it and learning what the format communicates naturally and what still needs explanation.

But the larger project is already clear to me.

I do not want to build one giant timeline of everything.

I want to build a place where different arguments about history can be made in streams.

History, seen in streams.
