Every Map Chooses What Deserves Space

Maps do not simply contain information. They decide what becomes large, what becomes small, what remains visible and what disappears. A Histomap turns those decisions into part of the argument.

J. Hunter Sizemore

Published August 2026

Every map begins by making exclusions.

A street map may give a road more visual weight than a river. A political map may make a border feel more permanent than the communities that cross it. A world map may centre one region and push another toward the edge. Even before labels are added, the geometry is already telling the reader what matters.

That is not a defect. It is what maps do.

The important question is whether those choices are visible enough to examine.

Space is an argument

When something receives more space on a page, screen or chart, it usually receives more attention as well. Size suggests importance. Continuity suggests persistence. Proximity suggests relationship. Separation suggests difference.

Conventional maps often make those choices feel natural because we are accustomed to their visual grammar. Land is land. Water is water. A border is a line. A capital gets a dot. The design decisions disappear into familiarity.

A Histomap is harder to mistake for a neutral container because its argument is exposed in the shape of the map itself.

If one historical stream widens while another narrows, the map is making a claim. If a stream continues through a political rupture rather than ending at it, that is a claim. If two traditions merge, split or remain separate, that too is a claim.

Every map chooses what deserves space. A Histomap should tell you why.

Width needs a meaning

This is why one of the basic rules of the Histomap format is that width must mean something.

John B. Sparks used changing stream widths to express his interpretation of the relative power of contemporary states, nations and empires. That measure was qualitative and historically situated. It gave his chart extraordinary visual force, but it also embedded his judgments directly into the page.

A different Histomap can define width differently. Population might be quantitative. The spread of a language might combine population and geographic reach. A map of political movements might use membership, institutional control or electoral strength. A map of domesticated species could use cultivated area, dietary importance or another explicitly stated measure.

The point is not that there is one correct metric. The point is that the metric should be declared rather than hidden.

Omission matters too

Space is also created by leaving things out.

No historical map can show everything. The decision to include one civilization, institution, person or event necessarily competes with other possible subjects. At small scale, the map has to suppress detail simply to remain readable. At large scale, it can reveal more.

Interactive Histomaps make that negotiation more flexible. Semantic zoom can allow a broad overview to remain legible while events, people, annotations and competing interpretations appear as the reader moves closer. But interaction does not remove editorial judgment. It only gives the map more levels on which to make it.

Make the choices contestable

The long-term aim of Histomaps.org is not to make those judgments disappear. It is to make them increasingly inspectable.

A reader should eventually be able to ask why a stream is this wide, why it begins here, why it changes name, why it splits, why an event is included, and what sources or assumptions support those choices.

That is where the format becomes more interesting than a decorative timeline. The geometry becomes something you can argue with.

A good Histomap should not merely look authoritative. It should reveal enough of its construction that the reader can disagree intelligently.
